1. Q1: Data analysis and insights (parents and online gaming)

The question: What meaningful insights can we derive from the survey data about parents' perceptions of online gaming, and how can we communicate them effectively to a wider audience? Then convert it into a social-media-ready infographic.

One-line answer: Parents fear online gaming more than they understand it. Worry and family fights rise together, and more rules did not mean fewer fights. What helped was curiosity: parents who understand why their child plays feel strain less often. So we speak to parents with calm and one small action, not with fear.

1.1 The data and how I cleaned it

  • Source: YourDOST parent survey (Google Form), 1–3 Dec 2020, 100 responses, 37 questions.
  • Duplicate: row 21 was an exact copy of row 18, with every answer identical, sent 9 minutes later. I removed it, leaving 99 unique responses.
  • The base: parents who answered "No" to Q1 ("Does your kid play online games?") never saw the rest of the form. So 66 parents (Yes or Sometimes) answered everything, and every % is out of 66. Reach: 66 of 99 = 2 in 3 (67%) say their kids play.
  • Grouping: the scales are No / Low / Medium / High. I count Medium + High as "worried" or "feels the impact", because Low is a passing thought. True/False answers are shown as Yes/No. "Rules used" is how many of the 6 listed rules a parent ticked (certain days, certain times, fixed duration, no gaming in exams, certain games only, spending cap).
  • Scores: No = 0, Low = 1, Medium = 2, High = 3. The fear score is the average of the 5 fear items; the family impact score is the average of the 3 impact items (frustration, arguments, strain).
  • Checks: every number is a live formula in the sheet (tab 3) on the clean data (tab 5), and I cross-checked the numbers in Python.
  • Tool: Numiqo (numiqo.com), an online statistics package, at significance level α = 0.05.

Workbook tabs ("Jamun"): 1 Read Me · 2 Numiqo Analysis (the centre of the work) · 3 Insights & Maths · 4 Colour Code · 5 Clean Data (66) · 6 Why This Story.

1.5 What the tests told me (the five findings)

  1. Fear, not gaming time, goes with conflict: ρ = 0.58 vs 0.17 for how often kids play.
  2. More rules did not mean fewer fights: rules × arguments χ² p = 0.21; rules × impact ρ = 0.06; Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.25. Yet 97% say rules are needed.
  3. Curiosity is the way out: understanding why the child plays goes with strain of 48% vs 79% (p = 0.04). Parents who have played games have high addiction fear 44% vs 76% (p = 0.01) and a fear score of 2.18 vs 2.46.
  4. Playing together is a start, not the fix: co-play × strain p = 0.79, so the design gives three curious questions, not "just play with them".
  5. Honest limits: 66 parents; not a random sample (clusters in Kolkata and Bangalore; 64% mothers, 42 of 66); data from Dec 2020. Every result is a link, not a cause. I say "goes with", never "causes".

1.7 How to take it to a wider audience

  • Format: one feed post (the infographic) plus a 5-slide carousel, one step per slide, linked by a yellow line and swipe hints.
  • Funnel (AIDA): Attention: the 94% hook → Interest: the loop → Desire: "curiosity breaks the loop" → Action: try tonight, then talk to an expert.
  • Audiences: worried parents (mothers of 6–12-year-olds first), fathers, schools (parent workshops), employers and HR (working parents), media (a press note).
  • Channels: Instagram feed and carousel, WhatsApp parent groups (where parents already talk about this), LinkedIn for HR and schools, YourDOST's corporate and campus partners.
  • Measure: reach and saves → quiz or workshop sign-ups → expert bookings. A/B test the hook: "You're not alone" vs "More rules didn't mean fewer fights". A new survey wave would refresh the 2020 data.

1.8 Likely Q1 questions

Why is the base 66 and not 100?
One exact duplicate came out, leaving 99. Parents who said their kids don't play never saw the other questions (form logic), so the 66 who answered everything are the base. Using 99 would understate every %.
Why Medium + High?
Low is a passing thought; Medium and High are a real worry. I also report High alone: addiction fear is 56% High. I chose the definition before looking at results, and I use it the same way everywhere.
Why Spearman and Mann-Whitney, not Pearson and t-tests?
The answers are ordinal (No/Low/Medium/High), and n is small, so I used rank-based, non-parametric tests that don't assume normality.
Is 48% vs 79% really significant?
χ² p = 0.042, but Fisher's exact p = 0.068 because only 14 parents don't understand why their child plays. So I call it moderate evidence. The stronger support is "played games → less high fear" (p = 0.011 on both χ² and Fisher, V = 0.31).
Did you show causation?
No. These are associations from one cross-sectional, non-random survey. The design says "goes with", and the disclaimer is printed on the image.
What's the most surprising insight?
97% believe in rules, but rules show no link to fewer fights (ρ = 0.06), while fear shows the strongest link (ρ = 0.58). The problem is the parent's fear, not the screen time.
What would you do next?
Run a new survey wave with a larger, random sample across cities; A/B test the two hooks; track saves, then sign-ups, then bookings; and build a "Gaming Parent" quiz plus a school parent workshop.

Likely Q2 questions

How did you choose the segments?
STP. B2B corporate is YourDOST's core revenue, so two corporate posts target the buyer (HR) and two target the user (employees). Students and young professionals are the largest individual segments. High achievers and the Diwali moment widen reach.
How would you measure success?
Saves and shares over likes, since they signal real value; comments on the question posts; profile visits and link clicks; and for B2B posts, demo or HR enquiries. Post at the listed times, test hooks, and double down on formats that are saved.
Why are these original?
Formats borrowed from everyday UI: a CAPTCHA, live captions, an expedition chart, a phone notification. Each one makes the point without saying "mental health" in the hook, which lowers stigma.
What about safety?
The tone is calm, not alarming. Crisis replies go to Tele MANAS 14416 and the clinical team. Stats carry a source line, and names and figures inside the mock UIs are illustrative.

Slide 1: "You backed the founder. Who backs them?"

Open on the photo: 11:47 pm, payroll on Friday, board meeting on Monday. Every investor has a founder like this. A private coach founders choose for themselves; the fund simply opens the door.

Slide 2: The program in 30 seconds

A coach for stress, loneliness and co-founder fights. Vision: every founder has a coach, just as every founder has a CA. Stage: already running, used by Upekkha and NSRCEL (IIM Bangalore) founders. Traction: 250+ founders coached. Price: ₹1,000–1,500 a week, paid by the founder. Ask: one introduction, then review at 90 days.

Slide 3: The problem

  • 49% of entrepreneurs have lived with a mental-health condition (Freeman et al. 2019).
  • 42.9% of Indian founders score low on wellbeing (WHO-5), and only 15.1% of the startup ecosystem ever gets professional help (Merak Ventures, State of Mind, 823 respondents).
  • 57% of early-stage founders feel well vs 74% of founders with six-plus years (YourDOST Emotional Wellbeing of Entrepreneurs study 2024, with INK, TiE, Upekkha and NSRCEL).
  • Co-founder conflict is behind 65% of failed high-potential startups (Noam Wasserman, HBS, about 10,000 founders).
  • Why now: Indian tech funding fell 17% to US$10.5 bn in 2025 (Tracxn), so founders wait longer to raise. Poor mental health costs Indian employers about US$14 bn a year (Deloitte 2022). US investors such as Freestyle Capital began paying for founder therapy in 2020 (Axios).

Slide 4: The solution

Four steps: Assess (week 0, a 5-minute WHO-5 wellbeing check) → Match (a dedicated coach within 48 hours) → Talk (regular 40-minute calls, 4, 6 or 8 by plan, with worksheets in between) → Measure (periodic progress checks; psychologists and psychiatrists on call). Topics: co-founder conflict, stress and anxiety, negative thoughts, focus, relationships, confidence.

Trust design: the fund pays ₹0 and opens the door (one intro, a 10% perk). The founder chooses, pays privately and owns it. Only an anonymous, opt-in summary goes back to the fund, never names or notes. It is not a crisis line; emergencies are routed to 24×7 helplines.

Likely Q3 questions

Why should the founder pay, not the fund?
Ownership and trust. Founders won't open up about a fund-paid program the fund might monitor. Founder-pays also means zero budget friction for the fund, so the yes is easy. The fund still adds value through the intro and the 10% perk. Later, funds can sponsor it as a portfolio benefit; that is upside, not the base case.
Is 8–12% conversion realistic?
It is the main unproven assumption, and I say so on slide 8. That is why the next step is a 90-day, 3-partner pilot that measures it before scaling. The break-even plan needs about 12% by Year 3; at 8% the program still covers gross costs, just later.
How did you get ₹10,800?
A ₹7,700 average program before GST (a blend of the three published plans) × 1.4 programs a year, assuming 40% of founders buy a second program.
Why is TAM ₹414 Cr bottom-up but ₹151 Cr top-down?
They measure different things. Bottom-up counts every founder at the spend level; top-down counts only founders with low wellbeing who aren't getting help. Either way, the Year-3 plan (₹1 Cr) is a small, believable share.
Why not just use therapy apps or EAPs?
They're built for individuals or employees. Founders aren't employees of an EAP-covered company, and co-founder conflict and investor pressure need a founder-specific structure. Exec coaches cost 13× more and have no clinical back-up.
What's the biggest risk?
Low conversion and founder reluctance. Mitigations: partner-led trust, the free workshop as the entry point, anonymity, and a short 4-week plan as a low-commitment start. Also coach supply and quality: we reuse YourDOST's vetted expert pool.
What is HHI and why show it?
The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, the sum of squared market shares. About 530 across the market means it is fragmented, with no founder-only leader, so there is room for a category-defining program.
What would you do in the first 90 days as owner?
Sign 3 pilot partners (for example Upekkha, NSRCEL and one VC); run the workshops; track reach → sign-ups → completion → repeat; add joint co-founder sessions; then fix the pricing mix and CAC before scaling to 15 partners.
