Act as a senior strategy consultant, FMCG growth strategist, consumer behaviour expert, brand strategist and executive presentation designer.
I am redesigning an existing 10-slide presentation on Kishlay Foods for the Managing Director, Founder and senior leadership.
The current presentation contains research on:

* Consumer and market problems
* Manufacturing footprint and production capacity
* Product portfolio and SKU architecture
* Pack formats and price points
* Distribution network and channel economics
* Competitor benchmarking
* Porter's Five Forces
* Blue Ocean Strategy
* Ansoff Matrix
* Growth opportunities
* A 90-day execution roadmap

The research is useful, but the existing presentation reads like a collection of separate research slides. I want you to completely redesign the narrative, slide order, headlines, visual hierarchy, diagrams and speaker notes.
1. THE CENTRAL STORY
Build the entire presentation around one central business question:
"How can Kishlay Foods convert its manufacturing and distribution capabilities into more profitable, consumer-led growth?"
The presentation must tell one continuous story:

1. The consumer wants an affordable, tasty and accessible snack.
2. Delivering that experience consistently creates challenges in manufacturing, packaging, freshness, logistics and price.
3. Kishlay has developed a regional manufacturing and distribution model to serve this demand.
4. Its portfolio serves different consumer occasions and price points.
5. Its manufacturing capabilities provide an operating foundation, but capacity utilisation and SKU complexity matter.
6. Its distribution network creates reach, but outlet productivity and channel economics determine commercial success.
7. Competition and input costs put pressure on margins.
8. Kishlay must make a strategic choice about where to compete and differentiate.
9. That choice must translate into focused go-to-market initiatives.
10. The Founder’s Office must convert those initiatives into measurable execution.

Every slide must answer a question raised by the previous slide.
2. TARGET AUDIENCE
Design for a Founder, Managing Director or senior executive.
Assume the audience:

* Understands manufacturing, finance, distribution, business strategy and operations.
* Is analytically strong and comfortable with numbers.
* Does not necessarily know every consumer behaviour or marketing framework.
* Has limited time and wants the main business insight quickly.

The presentation should also be understandable to an intelligent 18–20-year-old who has no FMCG experience.
Explain complex concepts through simple examples, business logic and visual storytelling.
Do not oversimplify the analysis. Simplify the explanation.
3. REQUIRED 10-SLIDE STRUCTURE
SLIDE 1 — THE OPPORTUNITY
Headline:
"The ₹5 snack is a small purchase. Building the business behind it is not."
Purpose:
Introduce Kishlay and establish why its business model is worth examining.
Show only the most important verified metrics:

* Revenue and reporting period.
* Manufacturing capacity.
* Retail reach.
* Geographic presence.
* Product portfolio scale.

Use one large consumer/product visual and a maximum of five metric cards.
Opening narrative:
"When someone buys a ₹5 snack, they make a small purchase decision. Behind that decision is a much larger system involving sourcing, manufacturing, packaging, distribution and retailer economics."
End by asking:
"What problem is this business designed to solve?"
SLIDE 2 — THE CONSUMER PROBLEM
Headline:
"The consumer wants an affordable snack. Delivering it fresh and consistently is the challenge."
Purpose:
Explain the consumer need before discussing the company.
Show:

* Affordability.
* Freshness and product quality.
* Taste and regional preference.
* Availability.
* Convenience.
* Price sensitivity.

Explain the gap between consumer expectations and the operational difficulty of satisfying them.
Use a simple consumer need-gap diagram.
Do not claim these needs have been validated through primary research unless evidence exists. Label assumptions as hypotheses.
End with:
"So, how has Kishlay built a system to address this problem?"
SLIDE 3 — THE KISHLAY BUSINESS MODEL
Headline:
"Kishlay's answer: manufacture closer to the consumer, then distribute through multiple channels."
Purpose:
Explain the company's business model.
Show a simple left-to-right visual:
Raw materials → Manufacturing facilities → Product packaging → Distribution channels → Retailers → Consumers.
Show:

* Owned versus third-party manufacturing.
* Plant locations.
* Production capacity.
* Distribution channels.
* Regional operating footprint.

Explain the commercial logic of the model.
End with:
"Once the manufacturing and distribution system is in place, what products should it serve?"
SLIDE 4 — THE PRODUCT AND CONSUMER STRATEGY
Headline:
"One consumer base, many consumption occasions — and different price points."
Purpose:
Explain the product portfolio through consumer behaviour.
Start with consumption occasions:

* Impulse snacking.
* Evening snacking.
* Family sharing.
* Breakfast and tea occasions.
* On-the-go consumption.
* Regional taste preferences.

Map each occasion to:

* Consumer need.
* Purchase motivation.
* Product category.
* Preferred pack size.
* Relevant price point.

Then show the product portfolio architecture:

* Savoury snacks.
* Namkeen and noodles.
* Bakery.
* Pantry products.
* Beverages and other categories.

Explain why portfolio breadth may create both distribution opportunities and SKU complexity.
End with:
"But serving many occasions and price points creates a manufacturing challenge."
SLIDE 5 — THE OPERATING ENGINE
Headline:
"The factory can produce at scale. The question is how efficiently that capacity is used."
Purpose:
Explain manufacturing capabilities and operating constraints.
Show:

* Manufacturing sites and installed capacities.
* Actual production, if available.
* Capacity utilisation.
* Production lines.
* Quality-control process.
* Relevant operational constraints.

Separate owned facilities from third-party manufacturing.
Show installed capacity and actual output for the same period.
Explain:

* Why capacity utilisation matters.
* How product mix affects production.
* Why manufacturing capacity alone does not guarantee sales growth.

End with:
"Even after manufacturing the product, the business must deliver it profitably."
SLIDE 6 — DISTRIBUTION AND UNIT ECONOMICS
Headline:
"Reaching 2 lakh outlets is an achievement. Getting more value from every outlet is the next challenge."
Purpose:
Explain how products reach consumers and where economics are created or lost.
Show:
Manufacturer → C&F/stockist → Distributor → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer.
Include:

* Retail reach.
* Geographic presence.
* Channel mix.
* Manufacturer realisation.
* Trade margins.
* Distribution economics.
* Sales per active outlet, if comparable data exists.

Distinguish clearly between:

* Revenue.
* Manufacturer realisation.
* Gross margin.
* Contribution margin.
* Profit.

Show the difference between distribution reach and distribution productivity.
End with:
"Every shelf position is contested, which brings us to the competitive landscape."
SLIDE 7 — COMPETITION AND INDUSTRY PRESSURE
Headline:
"Kishlay competes in a crowded market where consumers can switch brands easily."
Purpose:
Explain the competitive environment.
Group competitors by:

* National snack brands.
* Established Indian snack brands.
* Regional and value brands.
* Bakery and biscuit brands.
* Local and unorganised alternatives.

Compare:

* Product categories.
* Price points.
* Consumer positioning.
* Distribution.
* Brand differentiation.

Then introduce Porter's Five Forces:

1. Competitive rivalry.
2. Supplier bargaining power.
3. Buyer bargaining power.
4. Threat of substitutes.
5. Threat of new entrants.

Show one conclusion and one supporting piece of evidence per force.
Avoid comparing financial metrics across companies unless reporting periods and definitions are comparable.
End with:
"If the entry-price market is crowded, where can Kishlay build a stronger position?"
SLIDE 8 — THE STRATEGIC CHOICE
Headline:
"The next opportunity may not be more products. It may be a more focused portfolio and stronger regional differentiation."
Purpose:
Present the strategic recommendation.
Use Blue Ocean Strategy and the Ansoff Matrix as supporting frameworks.
Evaluate four growth directions:

1. Market penetration.
2. Market development.
3. Product development.
4. Diversification.

Recommend a prioritised sequence:
Priority 1:
Improve productivity in existing markets through stronger availability, hero SKUs, distribution efficiency and outlet productivity.
Priority 2:
Test regional and premium products where there is a defensible consumer proposition.
Priority 3:
Expand selectively into new markets and channels after validating demand and unit economics.
Show what the company should:

* Eliminate.
* Reduce.
* Raise.
* Create.

Do not present hypothetical market white spaces as proven opportunities. Identify what must be validated.
End with:
"How do we turn this strategic choice into commercial execution?"
SLIDE 9 — GO-TO-MARKET EXECUTION
Headline:
"Turn the growth opportunity into three measurable commercial pilots."
Purpose:
Translate the strategy into practical initiatives.
Create three workstreams:

1. General Trade:
Improve hero SKU availability, outlet productivity, replenishment and distribution efficiency.
2. Modern Trade:
Evaluate retailer economics, assortment, shelf placement, promotional costs and store-level productivity.
3. Quick Commerce:
Evaluate consumer occasions, pack-price fit, product conversion, repeat purchases, platform economics and fulfilment costs.

Treat exports as a separate market-entry feasibility initiative if relevant.
For each workstream, use the following sequence:
A. Need-gap analysis.
B. 5C analysis.
C. Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning.
D. Seven Ps marketing mix.
E. Pilot design.
F. Success metrics.
Every initiative must include:

* Business hypothesis.
* Target consumer.
* Target geography.
* Selected products.
* Channel.
* Investment or cost assumptions.
* Owner.
* Timeline.
* KPIs.
* Scale, modify or stop criteria.

End with:
"This is how the Founder’s Office can turn a strategy into measurable outcomes."
SLIDE 10 — THE FOUNDER'S OFFICE 90-DAY PLAN
Headline:
"My first 90 days: understand, prioritise, execute and scale."
Divide into:
Days 1–30: Diagnose.

* Understand the company and leadership priorities.

Days 31–60: Prioritise.

Days 61–90: Execute.

* Start an approved pilot.
* Track performance.
* Conduct weekly reviews.
* Resolve cross-functional blockers.
* Recommend whether to scale, modify or stop.

Include one integrated Founder’s Office dashboard with:

* Revenue.
* Sales by channel.
* SKU performance.
* Outlet productivity.
* Gross margin and contribution.
* Inventory and stock-outs.
* Pilot performance.
* Strategic initiative tracker.

Close with:
"The objective is not simply to grow revenue. It is to build repeatable, profitable growth through better consumer understanding, stronger distribution productivity and disciplined execution."
4. STORYTELLING RULES
These rules are mandatory.

1. Each slide must have one central message.
2. The headline must communicate the conclusion, not merely name the topic.
3. Each slide must answer a question created by the previous slide.
4. Each slide must end with a transition to the next slide.
5. Do not introduce a framework before explaining the business problem it helps solve.
6. Explain every major number in terms of its business implication.
7. Move from consumer need to business model, then operations, commercial economics, competition, strategy and execution.
8. Do not overload slides with facts that do not support the central argument.
9. Use consumer examples to explain marketing concepts.
10. Clearly distinguish facts, estimates, assumptions and recommendations.

Create a premium executive strategy presentation.

6. DATA INTEGRITY
Use the existing presentation as the primary source of company-specific information.
Do not invent numbers, company facts, consumer survey results, market shares, financial performance or customer insights.
Before redesigning, perform a data consistency audit.
Specifically check:

* Whether 2,600 MT/month represents capacity or actual production.
* Whether 50–75% capacity utilisation is consistent with the stated production.
* The difference between 236 catalogue products and 900+ claimed SKUs.
* Whether revenue, production and outlet productivity figures refer to comparable periods.
* Whether owned and third-party manufacturing capacities are separated.
* Whether the ₹10 distribution waterfall uses actual or estimated trade margins.
* Whether competitor financial comparisons use consistent periods and definitions.
* Whether historical financial information is incorrectly presented as current.
* Whether every source supports the claim attached to it.

Label every data point as:

1. Company-reported fact.
2. External source or estimate.
3. Historical figure.
4. Analyst calculation.
5. Strategic hypothesis.

If a number cannot be verified, flag it for validation rather than silently correcting it.
