WHO BUYS AND WHERE THEY LOOK (90 seconds). “I segment by what shoppers do and why before age and income, because behaviour tells us the pack and the shelf. Four shoppers. The planner mother stocks up monthly, compares price per gram and buys for guests; she is 35 to 45, the decision maker, in homes earning ₹30,000 to ₹1 lakh a month. She buys the 400-gram bhujia and, if we give her one, a 1 kg family pack. The child with a parent spots a bright pack at eye level and asks; the parent trades up to a multipack, so Rings with water toys and 6-packs of noodles. The young snacker, Gen Z, buys on impulse, wants heat and new flavours and is proud of Assam, which is Jolokia Chanachur and Jhalmuri, and also orders on apps. The host buys for festivals and guests: party boxes and gift tins, in homes at ₹1 to 1.5 lakh a month. These are hypotheses until we run 300 shopper intercepts. Price ladder: ₹10 to try, ₹55 every day, ₹60 kids’ packs, ₹110 take-home, ₹120 party, ₹199 gift, ₹249 bulk value. On the right, the shelf. An Indian woman’s eye is at about 140 cm, so 130 to 160 cm holds the anchors and Jolokia. A child of 6 to 10 sees at 105 to 128 cm, so 100 to 130 cm is for Rings, Noodles and Jhalmuri. Heavy value packs go low. Festive tins go top and on end caps for Bihu, Puja and Diwali. The evidence: national brands hold eye level today and Vishal’s own label sits beside them at 7 to 13% more per gram. The Wharton study by Drèze, Hoch and Purk found shelf location matters more than adding facings. And when a pack is missing, 31% of shoppers go to another store and 26% switch brand, so a 95% fill rate is part of the plan, not an afterthought.” Engagement: “Have we ever done a shelf audit or shopper intercepts in Guwahati stores? I would start the first week.” Transition: “So what exactly goes on that shelf?”
