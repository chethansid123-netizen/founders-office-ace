WHO BUYS AND WHERE THEY LOOK (90 seconds). “I group shoppers by what they do and why before age and income, because behaviour tells us the pack and the shelf. Read the table across. The planner mother stocks up monthly and checks price per gram; she wants trust, value and enough for guests; she buys the 400 gram bhujia and, if we give her one, a 1 kg family pack. She is 35 to 45, the decision maker, in homes earning ₹30,000 to ₹1 lakh a month. The child spots a bright pack and asks; the parent trades up to a multipack, so Rings with water toys and 6 packs of noodles. The young snacker, Gen Z, buys on impulse, wants heat and new flavours and is proud of Assam: Jolokia Chanachur and Jhalmuri, also ordered on apps. The host buys for festivals and guests: party boxes and gift tins, in homes earning ₹1 to 1.5 lakh a month. These are hypotheses until we run 300 shopper intercepts. The price ladder runs from ₹10 to try, ₹55 every day, ₹60 kids’ packs, ₹110 family, ₹120 party, ₹199 gift, to ₹249 for bulk value. On the right, the shelf. An Indian woman’s eyes are at about 140 cm, so 130 to 160 cm holds the anchors and Jolokia. A child of 6 to 10 sees at 105 to 128 cm, so 100 to 130 cm is for Rings, Noodles and Jhalmuri. Heavy value packs go low. Gift tins go top and on festival end caps. The evidence: national brands hold eye level today, and Vishal’s own label sits beside them at 7 to 13% more per gram. The Wharton study by Drèze, Hoch and Purk found shelf location matters more than adding facings. And when a pack is missing, 31% of shoppers go to another store and 26% switch brand, so a 95% fill rate is part of the plan.” Engagement: “Have we ever done a shelf audit or shopper intercepts in Guwahati stores? I would start in the first week.” Transition: “So what exactly goes on that shelf?”
