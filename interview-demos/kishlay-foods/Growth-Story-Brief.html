The Growth Story: deck brief

How can Kishlay Foods convert its manufacturing and distribution capabilities into more profitable, consumer-led growth?

Companion to Kishlay-Growth-Story.pptx / .pdf (9 slides). Prepared by Chethan N. Covers outputs B–H of the master prompt: slide-by-slide spec, storyline, data checks, research assumptions, and the opening and closing speeches.

BOverall storyline

One question runs through all nine slides. Each slide ends with a line that sets up the next one. The frameworks (Porter, ERRC, Ansoff, 5C, STP, 7Ps) only come in after the problem is clear.

1 Opportunity→2 Consumer problem→3 Business model→4 Products and occasions→5 Operating engine→6 Distribution and unit economics→7 Competition: three battles→8 Strategic choice→9 Three pilots and close
ActSlidesWhat the panel should believe by the end
Why it exists1–2A ₹5 snack is easy to buy and hard to deliver well: it has to be cheap, fresh, available and taste local.
How it works3–6Kishlay solved this with plants near the consumer, a ₹5–₹250 pack range and 2 lakh+ outlets. The open questions are how much of the plant is used and how much each outlet sells.
The pressure7The entry-price shelf is crowded and inputs take most of the cost, so more volume alone won’t fix margins.
What next8–9Focus the portfolio, test regional premium, prove it with three measured pilots, and close on the 90-day plan in the spoken close.

ASlide-by-slide specification

The speaker notes below are short summaries. The full script for each slide is in the PPTX notes pane.

01 · The opportunity

Headline
The ₹5 snack is a small purchase. Building the business behind it is not.
Purpose
Hook the panel and set the one question the deck answers.
Content
Five metric tiles: ₹344 Cr revenue (FY24), 2,600 MT installed capacity a month, 2 lakh+ outlets, 17+ states, 236 products. Data-label legend. Turmeric card with the central question.
Layout
Headline and lead on the left, Panda mascot on a red ellipse on the right, tiles in one row, question card below.
Visual
Kishlay mascot and pack photos; label colour on each tile.
Data required
Audited FY24/FY25 revenue; installed capacity by site with its period; active outlet count.
Speaker notes
“One coin buys the pack. Behind it are three plants, 2 lakh shops and 236 products. My question: how does Kishlay turn that machine into more profitable, consumer-led growth?”
Transition
What problem is this business designed to solve?

02 · The consumer problem

Headline
The consumer wants an affordable snack. Delivering it fresh and consistently is the challenge.
Purpose
Start from the shopper, not the factory.
Content
Six rows pairing what the consumer needs with what that demands of the business: affordability vs input costs (70–75% of cost), freshness vs 1–12 month shelf life, regional taste, availability, convenience, price sensitivity.
Layout
Left: three ₹5 packs with “A ₹5 coin, a hungry moment, a kirana counter.” Right: need → difficulty rows.
Visual
Real pack photos; arrow rows.
Data required
Input-cost share (CRISIL); shelf life by category (company); delivery radius per depot.
Speaker notes
“Each need sounds simple. Each one creates a hard operating problem: hold a ₹5 price when inputs are three-quarters of cost, stay fresh with short shelf lives, taste local in 17 states.”
Transition
How has Kishlay built a system to solve this?

03 · The business model

Headline
Kishlay’s answer: make it near the consumer, sell through many channels.
Purpose
Show the system end to end before going into its parts.
Content
Six-step chain: Inputs → Make (3 sites, 5 units) → Pack (₹5–₹250) → Channels → Retailers (2 lakh+) → Consumer (17+ states). Owned vs partner capacity (Guwahati own 2,000 MT; Hajipur and Chhattisgarh partners 300 MT/month each). Three “why it works” tiles.
Layout
Step strip on top, hub-and-spoke infographic on the left, capacity bars and why-it-works tiles on the right.
Visual
Your hub-and-spoke infographic; capacity bars.
Data required
Partner contract terms; per-site capacity period; channel mix.
Speaker notes
“Own the hub, rent capacity near demand, and push through distributors to 2 lakh shops. Tulsi Speciality Foods, incorporated 7 Oct 2024, is the newest piece.”
Transition
Once the system is in place, which products should it serve?

04 · Products and occasions

Headline
One consumer, many occasions — a price point for each.
Purpose
Link the range to consumer moments, then name the cost of breadth.
Content
Six occasions with pack photo, motive, products and price: Impulse ₹5, On the go ₹5–10, Evening chai ₹10–55, Family sharing ₹20–110, Breakfast and tea ₹5–55, Regional taste ₹5–55. 236 products by family (145 / 45 / 22 / 19 / 5). Breadth helps vs breadth costs (236 listed · 292+ claimed · 900+ with sizes). “Price never moves, grams do.”
Layout
Occasion table on the left (two-thirds), family bars and breadth cards on the right.
Visual
Real packs: rings, Zing noodles, 40 Aana, Paneer Bhujia, butter cookies, Jhalmuri, Masala Noodles (+25% extra).
Data required
Sales by SKU and pack size; one agreed SKU definition.
Speaker notes
“The occasion mapping is my hypothesis. The three SKU counts use different definitions, so I treat them as a range to validate, not as one fact.”
Transition
But serving many occasions and price points creates a manufacturing challenge.

05 · The operating engine

Headline
The factory can produce at scale. The question is how much is used.
Purpose
Separate installed capacity from actual output, and explain why utilisation matters.
Content
Plant photo and site table. Capacity 2,600 MT/month vs estimated output 1,300–1,900 (50–75% used; calculation). “At half use, every kg carries twice the fixed cost.” Limits: four separate lines, product mix, shelf life, people (512 staff, absence peaks in July). Six quality checks; ISO 22000, FSSAI, HACCP.
Layout
Three columns: plant and sites · capacity vs output · what limits it.
Visual
Aerial plant photo; two bars; certification logos.
Data required
Monthly production by line; realisation per kg; changeover hours.
Speaker notes
“Actual output isn’t published. ₹344 Cr ÷ ₹150–220 a kilo gives roughly 50–75% use. Two caveats: the revenue is FY24 while the capacity is today’s, and revenue includes co-packing.”
Transition
Even once it’s made, the product has to be delivered — profitably.

06 · Distribution and unit economics

Headline
Reaching 2 lakh outlets is an achievement. More value per outlet is the next one.
Purpose
Show where the ₹10 goes, and move the conversation from reach to productivity.
Content
Chain strip. ₹10 waterfall: GST ₹0.48 (calculated), shop ₹1.60 and trade ₹0.70 (estimates), about ₹7.22 to the company. Five numbers, five meanings: revenue, realisation, gross margin, contribution (not public), net profit (FY23 −3.4%, FY22 −6.5%). Reach vs productivity: ₹17k a year per outlet at factory price ≈ ₹24k at MRP ≈ 6.5 packs a day (upper bound).
Layout
Waterfall on the left, five-numbers table in the middle, reach vs productivity on the right.
Visual
Waterfall bars; label colours.
Data required
Actual trade-margin structure; active outlets; sales per outlet by beat.
Speaker notes
“₹344 Cr across 2 lakh outlets is at most ₹17k each a year at factory price. Not every outlet is active, so this is an upper bound. I haven’t compared this with peers because there’s no like-for-like data.”
Transition
Every shelf slot is contested — which brings us to competition.

07 · Competition and industry pressure

Headline
Three battles on one crowded ₹5 shelf.
Purpose
Show every competitor by the battle it fights, where each meets Kishlay by category and price, and the five forces with evidence.
Content
Battle 1, national: PepsiCo, ITC, Nestlé, Britannia, Parle, Haldiram’s, Bikaji, Mrs. Bectors. Battle 2, East and NE (the key fight): PRAN, Yellow Diamond, Gopal, Balaji, Anmol, Bisk Farm, Annapurna, Wai Wai, Crax, Too Yumm!, Munchh Onn. Battle 3, local and loose: Motu Rama, Chittchore Namkeenz, local bakeries, chanachur, street food. Each has a line on Kishlay’s answer. Category map with eight rows (chips, rings, bhujia, noodles, cookies, cakes, loose, online/D2C) and the only confirmed prices: Lay’s ₹5, Yellow Diamond ₹5, Crax ₹5, Wai Wai ₹5, Britannia cookies ₹35, Haldiram’s bhujia ₹110. Five-force meters: Rivalry, Suppliers and Buyers High; Substitutes Medium-high; Entrants Medium.
Layout
Three battle cards on top; category map bottom-left; five-force meters bottom-right.
Visual
16 brand logos, Kishlay pack thumbnails, animated force meters.
Data required
Competitor price lists by pack; shelf share in sample outlets.
Speaker notes
Partner and rival (PepsiCo’s Frito-Lay and Britannia co-pack with Kishlay); look-alike disputes (Mrs. Bectors, Motu Rama); D2C peers are benchmarks only. No competitor factory or distributor comparisons, because none are verified.
Transition
If the ₹5 shelf is this crowded, where can Kishlay build a stronger position?

08 · The strategic choice

Headline
Break away from the ₹5 crowd: taste, premium, reach.
Purpose
Show on one chart why Kishlay looks like every ₹5 rival today, and where it should move.
Content
Blue Ocean strategy canvas across seven factors (low price, range breadth, ad spend, made local, regional taste, premium packs, shelf reach) with four curves: national brands, regional value, Kishlay today, Kishlay proposed. ERRC chips under the factors. Ansoff in order: P1 penetrate, P2 regional premium, P3 new markets once proven, no new categories. Three things to validate first.
Layout
Canvas on the left; Ansoff and the validation list on the right.
Visual
Curves fade in one after another, ending on the dashed “proposed” line.
Data required
Shopper research to replace the judgement scores; SKU velocity; price tests.
Speaker notes
“Kishlay today sits almost on top of the regional value curve. That is a red ocean. The dashed line is where I’d take it.” The curve heights are judgement, labelled on the slide.
Transition
How do we turn this choice into commercial execution?

09 · Go-to-market execution

Headline
Three channel pilots, 12 weeks, one rule: scale, modify or stop.
Purpose
Make the strategy testable, with a timeline, owners and decision points.
Content
Method chips (Need gap → 5C → STP → 7Ps → Pilot → KPIs). 12-week Gantt: general trade weeks 1–8 (Head of Sales NE), modern trade weeks 1–12 (modern-trade lead), quick commerce weeks 5–12 (Founder’s Office), each ending in a decision point. Three pilot cards covering hypothesis, who, products, key P, KPI chips, the scale/stop rule and the costs to size, with pack photos. Exports handled as a separate feasibility study.
Layout
Chips, Gantt, then three cards.
Visual
Bars grow, decision diamonds pop, traffic-light dots.
Data required
Baseline beat sales; listing and promoter costs; platform fees.
Speaker notes
“No invented rupee costs. Each card names what has to be costed with finance.”
Transition
Closing line on the slide (“The hard part is built…”, then “Thank you.”), followed by the closing speech.

EOpening speech · 60–90 seconds

Good morning. When a child in Guwahati hands a ₹5 coin across a kirana counter for a pack of Non Stop rings, it looks like the smallest purchase in the economy. Behind it is a business most people never see: a plant in North Guwahati, partner plants in Hajipur and Chhattisgarh, around 2,600 tonnes of capacity a month, 236 products and more than two lakh shops across 17 states.

Kishlay has already solved the hard part: making an affordable, regional snack and getting it onto the shelf. So I didn’t ask how to grow. I asked a sharper question: how can Kishlay turn its manufacturing and distribution into more profitable, consumer-led growth?

In the next ten minutes I’ll walk through the consumer problem, how Kishlay’s system solves it, where the pressure is (plant use, outlet productivity and a crowded ₹5 shelf), and then three pilots and a 90-day plan to test what I’m proposing.

One note before I start: every number is labelled — company, external, historical, my own calculation, or hypothesis — so you can see exactly what I know and what I’d want to validate with you.

About 190 words, roughly 75 seconds at a calm pace.

FClosing speech · about 30 seconds

To sum up: Kishlay has built the hard things — plants near the consumer, a pack for every occasion, and reach into two lakh shops. The next stage of growth isn’t about adding more of everything. It is about selling more of the right products in the shops it already reaches, testing regional premium carefully, and measuring every move. The objective is not simply to grow revenue, but to build repeatable, profitable growth. I’d love to start on that with you — and I’d like to begin by asking which of these numbers you already track every week.

About 95 words, roughly 35 seconds.
