FIVE FORCES WITH THE NUMBERS (75–90 s). Say this first: “No one publishes five-forces scores, so I’ve rated each force on one rule — High means it is already cutting the margin, with numbers to show it.” Suppliers, High: Kishlay buys from potato farms and cold stores, palm-oil refiners, flour and sugar mills, and pouch makers. These inputs are 70–75% of cost per CRISIL. In FY25 palm oil rose about 54% and potato about 56%, and Gopal’s gross margin fell from 28.1% to 20.2% year on year in Q4 FY25. Kishlay itself lost ₹20.7 Cr in FY22 when wheat, sugar and palm oil spiked. Buyers, High: super-stockists, distributors, two lakh-plus shops and shoppers paying in coins. Of every ₹10 the trade keeps about ₹2.30 and GST takes 48 paise, so about ₹7.22 comes back. Kishlay sells about ₹17k a year per outlet against Gopal’s ₹29k — so a shop can swap Kishlay out. MRP can’t move from ₹5 and ₹10, and the GST cut from 12% to 5% in September 2025 goes to shoppers as more grams. Rivalry, High: Tracxn tracks 130-plus competitors and Ken Research counts about 2,500 players in savoury snacks; Gopal and Bikaji are four to seven times Kishlay’s revenue and earn 7.2% and 10.5% EBITDA against 2.5–4%; and Kishlay’s growth slowed from 32% in FY20 to 2.5% in FY24. New entrants, Medium: easy at town level with a fryer and a pouch machine, hard at 2,600 MT a month and two lakh outlets; quick commerce — Blinkit in Guwahati since January 2025 — is a new door that skips distributors. Substitutes, Medium-high: loose chanachur, jhalmuri and samosa, and copycats, which Kishlay has fought in the Gauhati High Court in 2019 and the Delhi High Court in 2026. The trade split is industry-typical, not Kishlay-specific.
