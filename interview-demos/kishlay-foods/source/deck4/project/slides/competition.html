COMPETITION AND INDUSTRY PRESSURE (75 s). “Every slot on that hanging strip is contested, and the shopper can switch for one coin. Five groups of rivals. National brands — PepsiCo’s Lay’s and Kurkure, ITC’s Bingo! and YiPPee!, Nestlé’s Maggi — sell chips, extruded snacks and noodles from ₹5 upward, on big-brand trust and national ad spend. Established Indian snack brands — Haldiram’s, Bikaji, Gopal and Balaji — own namkeen, bhujia and wafers on authentic taste and range depth. Regional and value players — Prataap’s Yellow Diamond, DFM’s Crax, Pran from Bangladesh, CG Foods’ Wai Wai — fight mostly at ₹5 on price and fun, strongest in the East and Northeast. Bakery and biscuit makers — Britannia, Parle, Bisk Farm, Anmol — own the daily staple with scale. And local, unorganised namkeen and bakeries sell fresh, by weight, with no brand cost. Kishlay spans all of these, from ₹5 to ₹250, and its edge is that it makes locally and owns regional flavours. Five forces, each with one piece of evidence: rivalry is high — Tracxn tracks 130-plus competitors; supplier power is high — raw materials are 70–75% of cost per CRISIL, and in FY25 palm oil rose about 54% and potato about 56% per Gopal’s results; buyer power is high — the price is fixed at ₹5 and ₹10 and the trade wants margin and credit; substitutes are medium-high — loose namkeen sits beside the packs; and new entrants are medium — easy at town level, hard at two lakh outlets. I’ve deliberately left out cross-company margins: Kishlay’s latest public figures are older and defined differently from Gopal’s and Bikaji’s, so the comparison wouldn’t be fair.” Transition: “If the entry-price shelf is crowded, where can Kishlay build a stronger position?”
