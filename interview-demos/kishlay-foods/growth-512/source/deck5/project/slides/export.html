ENGINE 2: EXPORTS (90 s). “Second engine: exports, nearest first, because freight and regulation get harder the further we go. Bhutan: Kishlay already sells there. I estimate about ₹3.1 Cr today — 1% of sales; growing 10% a year with more SKUs per importer gets to about ₹4.1 Cr. Week one is just a baseline: who the importers are, volumes and margins. Nepal is bigger and trickier. Eight trucks a month through Panitanki–Kakarbhitta into Eastern Nepal is about ₹12 Cr a year. But a 30% duty and 13% VAT leave about ₹19 a kilo — less than a ₹10 pack earns in India. That’s why Haldiram’s now makes locally with Anandam, and Bikaji formed a 50:50 joint venture with Chaudhary Group in 2025. So my plan: export to prove demand, then license a local maker for a royalty with no capital. Buyers I’d meet: Bhat-Bhateni with 27 stores, United Distributors of the Vishal Group reaching about 45,000 outlets, Nepal Overseas, Vhandar online and MV Dugar. The pilot is two trucks, ₹28.5 lakh, of which ₹25 lakh is stock that sells. The Gulf is where the money is: Haldiram’s 200-gram bhujia sells at AED 5.69 in Carrefour, about ₹146, against ₹55 here. There are 43 lakh Indians in the UAE, and PRAN from Bangladesh sells about $245 million a year there — proof that South Asian snacks travel. Buyers: Lulu, Al Adil, Nesto, Carrefour, noon, Asian Basket. The risk is real: shipping through Hormuz has been disrupted since February 2026, so we’d route via Fujairah, Khor Fakkan, Jeddah or Sohar, and decide go or no-go in week 13. Only ₹9.4 lakh is at risk before that decision.” Engagement: “Which Bhutan importers do you work with today, and do they also buy for Nepal?” Transition: “The third engine needs no new market at all — just the plants.”
